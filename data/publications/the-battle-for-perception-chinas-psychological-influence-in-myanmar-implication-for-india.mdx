Myanmar is strategically very important for China because of its geographical position which lies near China, India and the Indian Ocean. Beijing’s extensive economic presence, diplomatic engagement with Myanmar’s military authorities and influence over armed organisations provide it with considerable strategic leverage. Recent assessments describe China as an indispensable actor in Myanmar’s conflict and peace processes, while India seeks to protect its own security and connectivity interests through the [Act East Policy](https://www.mea.gov.in/distinguished-lectures-detail?840) and projects such as the [Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project]( https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2295662&reg=48&lang=2) and [India-Myanmar Thailand Trilateral Highway](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2295662&reg=48&lang=2). 

The argument, however, is that there is strong evidence of China’s broader cognitive and information warfare capabilities and of its extensive influence in Myanmar, but evidence of a specifically coordinated Chinese psychological campaign in Myanmar, directed exclusively against India, remains a more hazardous task to accomplish. The more defensible interpretation is that China’s influence architecture in Myanmar can generate significant psychological and strategic effects that constrain India’s regional influence. 

## Introduction

The contemporary competition between India and China is increasingly extending beyond conventional military and economic domains into the cognitive domain where perceptions, narratives, information and trust become instruments of strategic power. China’s military doctrine has long incorporated the concept of the “Three Warfares” – psychological warfare, public-opinion warfare and legal warfare. Contemporary Chinese thinking has subsequently developed the concept of cognitive-domain operations, which seeks to influence an adversary’s perceptions, decision making and behaviour through information and psychological mechanisms.

This development is particularly significant for India because China’s influence extends across India’s immediate neighbourhood, Myanmar which occupies a critical position in this competition. It provides China with access towards the Bay of Bengal and the Indian Ocean while simultaneously serving as India’s gateway to Southeast Asia. China’s infrastructure projects, economic relationships and diplomatic engagements have therefore acquired strategic significance.

The central argument of this commentary is that China’s strategic influence in Myanmar should be understood not merely through the prism of physical infrastructure or military diplomacy but also through the battle for perceptions and narratives. Beijing’s ability to cultivate political relationships, present itself as a stabilising power, influence information environments and demonstrate diplomatic leverage can indirectly weaken India’s strategic position. This is especially relevant to India’s Northeast, where security challenges, ethnic conflicts, insurgency and narcotics trafficking and cross border movement create an environment which is particularly susceptible to the competing narrative. India should be wary of these threats in advance and take measures to act on it responsibly. 

## China’s Cognitive Warfare Doctrine

China’s approach to psychological operations forms part of a broader strategic conception of information and cognitive warfare. The PLA’s “[Three Warfares](https://www.orfonline.org/research/china-s-three-warfares-strategy-in-action-implications-for-the-sino-india-boundary-the-arctic-and-antarctica)” framework combines psychological warfare, public opinion warfare and legal warfare to influence an adversary without necessarily resorting to conventional military force. Contemporary cognitive domain operations extend this objective towards influencing how individuals and societies perceive events and make decisions.  

For India, this represents a significant transformation in the nature of strategic competition. The objective is not necessarily to convince Indian audiences of a single Chinese narrative, but potentially to create uncertainty, weaken trust, amplify divisions and influence strategic decision-making in the process. Research shows how the Chinese have manipulated operations against India by use of selective information, diplomatic messaging, state-affiliated media and digital platforms to promote narratives about China while challenging India’s international image.

## Myanmar as a Strategic Psychological Battlefield

Myanmar’s geographical position makes it particularly valuable to Beijing. China seeks secure access to the Indian Ocean, while Myanmar provides an alternative route connecting China’s landlocked southwest to maritime corridors. Chinese investments in pipelines, ports and the China-Myanmar Economic Corridor consequently have strategic implications for India’s eastern neighbourhood. 

At the political level, China’s ability to engage with Myanmar’s military authorities and various armed actors gives Beijing a form of influence that India does not always possess to the same degree. Recent scholarship describes China as an indispensable partner in parts of Myanmar’s conflict environment, particularly around northern Shan State. This creates an important psychological effect: the perception that Beijing is indispensable to Myanmar’s political and security architecture becomes a source of power. 

## Narrative Power and the Image of China as Stabilising Actor

One of China’s important strategic advantages is its ability to portray itself as a pragmatic actor focused on stability, economic development and sovereignty. At a time when Myanmar faces international isolation and prolonged conflict, Beijing’s willingness to maintain relations with the country’s military led authorities provide Myanmar with an alternative diplomatic channel.

China’s engagement with Myanmar’s leadership in 2026 demonstrates this continuing approach. During [President Min Aung Hlaing’s state visit to China](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/china-embraces-myanmars-president-former-junta-chief-seeks-legitimacy-2026-06-16/), Beijing emphasised bilateral cooperation while maintaining its broader economic and strategic interests in Myanmar.

The psychological implication for India is significant. If Myanmar’s political and military elites increasingly perceive China as the external power capable of delivering economic resources, diplomatic support and conflict mediation, India’s ability to shape political outcomes may gradually diminish.

## Exploiting Existing Vulnerabilities in India’s Northeast

Psychological operations are most effective when they exploit pre-existing social, political and economic vulnerabilities. India’s Northeast contains several such fault lines, including ethnic tensions, insurgency, underdevelopment, migration concerns and border insecurity. This does not mean that China creates these problems. Rather, the strategic danger lies in the possibility that existing grievances can be amplified through digital narratives, selective information or disinformation. Research on Chinese influence activities in India has documented attempts to shape online discourse and exploit domestic political and social divisions.

For India’s Northeast, therefore, cognitive security cannot be separated from conventional security. A socially fragmented borderland is potentially more vulnerable to external influence than a socially resilient one.

## The India–China Competition over Connectivity

Infrastructure is another important component of the psychological competition. China’s China–Myanmar Economic Corridor and associated infrastructure projects create not only physical connectivity but also perceptions of Chinese economic indispensability. India, meanwhile, is attempting to strengthen its own connectivity through the Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project and India–Myanmar–Thailand Trilateral Highway. Consequently, connectivity becomes a form of strategic signaling. Successful Chinese projects can reinforce perceptions of China’s capacity to deliver infrastructure. Indian infrastructure projects have already started in the Indo-Myanmar Borderlands like Manipur, Nagaland. 

## Implications for India

India’s response cannot rely exclusively on counter-propaganda. A sustainable response requires strengthening the country’s narrative credibility and societal resilience.

First, India needs greater coordination between diplomatic, intelligence, military and civilian institutions in identifying foreign influence campaigns.

Second, border communities must become active participants in [India’s security architecture](https://idsa.in/publisher/journal-of-defence-studies/the-evolution-of-indias-national-security-architecture). Development, employment and connectivity can reduce vulnerabilities that external actors may exploit. The ethnically similar communities in the Indo-Myanmar borderlands needs more assimilation for securing the trust of these communities and making policies effective in the region. 

Third, India needs faster and more credible strategic communication, particularly during crises involving Manipur, Nagaland, Mizoram and other Northeast border regions.

Fourth, India’s own connectivity projects need greater implementation efficiency because infrastructure is not merely an economic instrument; it also shapes perceptions of India’s reliability as a regional power.

Finally, India should develop greater expertise in cognitive warfare, digital forensics, OSINT, strategic communication and counter-disinformation. The United Service Institution of India’s recent work similarly [argues](https://usiofindia.org/publication-details.php?id=1164) that cognitive warfare integrates psychology, information and technology to influence perception, trust and decision-making.

## Conclusion

People should increasingly view China’s use of psychological influence as part of its broader strategy of “winning without fighting.” Myanmar provides Beijing with an especially valuable strategic environment because of its geographical proximity to India, access to the Indian Ocean, internal political fragmentation and dependence on external economic and diplomatic partners.

The available evidence does not justify the simplistic conclusion that China is conducting a single, centrally coordinated psychological operation in Myanmar exclusively against India. Rather, the evidence points towards something strategically more complex: China’s broader information, diplomatic, economic and cognitive influence in Myanmar creates conditions that can indirectly constrain India’s strategic influence.

For India, the challenge is therefore not simply to counter Chinese propaganda. It is to build a credible alternative narrative of partnership in Myanmar and India’s Northeast – one based on connectivity, development, security cooperation, respect for local communities and reliable implementation. In the emerging competition between the two Asian powers, the decisive battlefield may increasingly be neither the mountains nor the maritime domain, but the perceptions and choices of people living between them.
