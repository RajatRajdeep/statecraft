The BRICS grouping has evolved from a loose economic acronym into a significant platform for emerging powers seeking greater voice in global governance. For India, the forthcoming summit under its presidency carries particular weight. It arrives at a moment of geopolitical flux—marked by conflicts in West Asia, shifting energy markets, intensifying great-power competition, and domestic imperatives of growth, equity and strategic autonomy. Four interlocking themes emerge from recent reflections on the subject: the enduring value of the Russia relationship within BRICS, the necessity of pragmatic engagement with China on shared interests, the urgent question of energy security, and the case for embedding a gender agenda in the grouping’s future work. Taken together, these perspectives outline a coherent Indian approach—one that prioritises resilience, selective cooperation and independent agency rather than alignment with any single power or ideology.

Russia remains one of the more distinctive pillars of India’s external relationships and a central actor inside BRICS. The bilateral partnership is rooted in a long history that includes Soviet support during critical moments of Indian state-building and has continued, with adaptations, into the post-Cold War and post-Ukraine eras. Moscow has consistently treated India as a strategic partner rather than a subordinate, recognising New Delhi’s preference for multipolarity and its resistance to bloc politics. Within BRICS, Russia brings energy resources, defence technology, diplomatic weight in West Asia and Central Asia, and a willingness to challenge Western institutional dominance. These assets complement India’s own priorities. Russian crude has helped cushion India against price spikes and supply disruptions; defence cooperation continues to underpin operational readiness even as India diversifies suppliers; and Moscow’s advocacy for greater use of national currencies and alternative payment systems aligns with New Delhi’s interest in reducing dollar dependence.

Yet the relationship is not without constraints. Russia’s deepening ties with China and its preoccupation with the European theatre limit the bandwidth available for South Asian concerns. India has therefore treated the partnership as a valuable but not exclusive pillar of its strategic autonomy. In the BRICS context this translates into support for practical cooperation—on energy, connectivity, and institutional reform—while resisting any attempt to turn the grouping into an anti-Western alliance. Russia’s presence helps balance the internal dynamics of BRICS, preventing any single member from dominating the agenda and reinforcing the idea that the platform can accommodate divergent national interests.

Engagement with China presents a sharper dilemma. The border dispute, competitive infrastructure initiatives, and differing visions of regional order create friction that cannot be wished away. At the same time, the two countries share concrete interests inside BRICS. Both favour a multipolar international system, greater representation for the Global South in international financial institutions, and mechanisms that reduce vulnerability to unilateral sanctions or dollar volatility. The New Development Bank and the Contingent Reserve Arrangement already offer modest alternatives to Western-dominated frameworks. Discussions on local-currency settlement, digital payment systems, and climate-related financing open further avenues for cooperation that do not require resolution of territorial differences.

China’s approach, however, is layered. Beijing seeks to harden BRICS into a more cohesive counterweight to Western institutions, promote the internationalisation of the renminbi, and shape the grouping’s external posture. India’s interest lies in keeping the agenda functional rather than ideological. Cooperation on disaster management, climate monitoring, supply-chain resilience, and technological standards can proceed without accepting hierarchical leadership or political alignment. The test for Indian diplomacy is to extract practical gains—finance, technology, market access—while preserving the freedom to pursue independent relationships with the United States, Europe, Japan and others. Strategic autonomy is not isolation; it is the capacity to engage multiple partners on issues of mutual benefit without subordination.

Energy security sits at the intersection of these geopolitical and economic calculations. India remains a major importer of crude oil and gas. Volatility in West Asia, combined with the long-term transition away from hydrocarbons, creates both immediate price risks and structural challenges. Domestic exploration has yielded modest results relative to demand; the geological probability of discovering another Mumbai High-scale field appears limited, and the commercial track record of public-sector companies has been mixed. High-risk, high-cost exploration in complex basins therefore offers uncertain returns.

A more durable approach focuses on the entire energy value chain rather than the wellhead alone. Securing supplies at the right price, in the right location, and at the right time requires diversified import sources, strategic petroleum reserves, efficient refining and distribution infrastructure, and accelerated investment in renewables, storage and grid modernisation. Public resources may be better deployed in building integrated partnerships—across LNG terminals, cross-border pipelines where feasible, and joint ventures in renewable manufacturing—than in speculative upstream drilling. BRICS can play a constructive role here. Member states control significant hydrocarbon reserves and are simultaneously investing in clean-energy technologies. Joint work on energy efficiency standards, critical-mineral supply chains, and financing for just transitions can reduce collective vulnerability. The grouping’s value lies less in grand declarations than in concrete projects that improve energy access and affordability for large populations.

Any serious discussion of development and security inside BRICS must also address gender. Economic growth that leaves half the population behind is neither sustainable nor just. A feminist political-economy lens reveals that energy transitions, infrastructure investment and financial architecture all have differentiated impacts on women and men. Women in many BRICS countries remain underrepresented in formal energy sectors, bear disproportionate burdens of unpaid care work, and face barriers to credit, land rights and skills training. Climate shocks and energy poverty hit them hardest.

India’s presidency offers an opportunity to institutionalise a gender perspective. This need not mean rhetorical commitments alone. Practical steps include integrating gender analysis into the design of New Development Bank projects, setting targets for women’s participation in energy and digital infrastructure, expanding care-economy financing, and creating monitoring mechanisms that track outcomes rather than inputs. A dedicated BRICS platform—building on existing women’s forums—could develop roadmaps for gender-responsive budgeting, skills programmes in renewable energy, and data systems that make inequalities visible. Such measures strengthen rather than dilute the grouping’s developmental mandate. Inclusive growth expands the domestic market, improves human capital, and enhances social resilience—all of which support strategic autonomy.

These four strands—Russia as a balancing partner, China as a selective collaborator, energy as a systems challenge, and gender as a structural priority—point toward a consistent Indian posture. BRICS should remain a flexible platform for issue-based cooperation among major non-Western economies. It should not become a military alliance, an ideological bloc, or a vehicle for any single member’s hegemony. India’s contribution lies in insisting on practical outcomes, transparent institutions, and respect for diverse national paths. The New Development Bank can expand its portfolio in sustainable infrastructure; payment-system experiments can reduce transaction costs; energy and climate working groups can share technology and finance; and a gender lens can ensure that the benefits of growth reach broader populations.

Success will be measured less by summit communiqués than by whether member states can deliver tangible improvements in economic resilience, energy access and social equity. For India, the test is whether engagement inside BRICS enhances rather than constrains its ability to pursue independent relationships and domestic priorities. In a world of contested multipolarity, that capacity for calibrated, interest-driven cooperation remains the most valuable strategic asset. The conversations already under way—on Russia’s role, China’s ambitions, energy systems and gender justice—provide the raw material for a more mature and effective Indian approach to the grouping in the years ahead.
