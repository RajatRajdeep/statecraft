The stunning outcome of US [midterm elections](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/11/03/us/elections/results-house.html) has dealt President Donald Trump a significant political setback. It also reflects that his extreme ideological agenda is favoured only by a minority of Americans. The scale of the Republican Party’s losses is staggering. It marks the most serious reversal of his tenure, fundamentally altering the domestic political environment in which his administration conducts domestic and foreign policy. The Democratic Party has secured control of the Senate and the House. Since the results are widely interpreted as a referendum on Trump’s erratic policies, they represent a broad expression of voter dissatisfaction with the administration’s economic and foreign-policy priorities. More importantly, they introduce a new institutional constraint on Trump’s international ambitions. This raises questions about the credibility, continuity and sustainability of American diplomatic commitments.

The electoral outcome of the Republican Party’s defeat is particularly consequential because Trump’s second presidency has been characterised by [transactional approach](https://ip-quarterly.com/en/transatlantic-relationship-strategic-necessity-trumps-values) to international relations. His policies have favoured personal diplomacy over institutional process and his administration has pursued an aggressive trade and tariff policy. This has affected both allies and rivals. While military pressure on Iran and its regional proxies has been intensified, American support for Ukraine amid war fatigue has been a key fractious issue. Trump has questioned America’s traditional security arrangements with European allies and sought to redefine Washington’s strategic engagement with India on narrower terms. Therefore, the Democratic control of the Senate and the House is likely to strengthen congressional scrutiny of presidential appointments, defence expenditure, diplomatic initiatives and international agreements. Now the Trump administration will face a more formidable opposition across both chambers on nearly every major issue. Although the President retains substantial constitutional authority over military operations and diplomatic negotiations, the US Congress can influence the implementation of Trump’s foreign policy through legislation and oversight.

The conflict involving Iran, Israel and the US is sure to be one of the most pressing challenges to this new political battle. The conflict has become a much larger international economic issue for all importers and exporters due to the military escalation, disruption of maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz and doubts regarding security and safety in the region. The repercussions will have a wider reach and will impact energy markets, shipping, insurance costs, fertiliser and food costs and the economic health of energy-importing nations. Washington also has to balance its military stance with Trump’s campaign commitment to not getting involved in prolonged American military engagements in other countries. A Democratic-led Congress might be more aggressive in questioning legal rationale in current war powers statutes, goals, expense and length of military operations and related risks. Although the congressional resistance does not necessarily mean a no-more-military option in the near term, it can be an additional pressure to push the White House to explain its goals and to seek a long-term diplomatic resolution acceptable to regional players.

The developments related to Iran have special strategic and economic importance for India because of the large expatriate community of India in the Gulf countries. New Delhi is highly [vulnerable to energy imports](https://mecouncil.org/publication/indias-gulf-exposure-economy-security-and-strategic-risk/), commercial ties with West Asia, particularly investments in its Chabahar port, maritime links, remittance transfers and other regional activities, which renders any prolonged instability particularly consequential. A prolonged crisis in the Strait of Hormuz would lead to higher costs for India’s energy imports and more trade deficit. Meanwhile, Washington’s capacity to have a unified regional approach despite domestic political storminess will have a broader impact on India’s overall interaction with the US, Israel, Iran and the Gulf nations. If congressional investigations into the costs of the war and growing worries over economic pressures at home continue to grow in the coming months, they may be more challenging for a politically vulnerable Trump administration to maintain a steady Middle East policy. This will, therefore, require New Delhi to exercise a measured and carefully calibrated diplomacy to ensure strategic independence and maintain positive relations with alternative regional partners.

The Ukraine war is another big policy issue. Trump has put considerable emphasis on talks with Moscow, pressure on Kyiv and potentially cutting back on American military aid, raising significant concern among the European allies, especially those on Russia’s doorstep. Democratic lawmakers will be pushing for continued security aid to Ukraine, for more intense interest in Russian sanctions policy and enforcement, and to have greater transparency of negotiations with the Kremlin and any territorial arrangements. Congress might also attempt to reopen and pass bipartisan Ukraine support bills that had previously been supported by the Senate, and tighten economic sanctions on Russia. But a Democratic majority cannot decide how the President will engage in the negotiations. The more likely scenario is a complicated American stance, not as an ideological contest, but one that involves a struggle between the White House’s diplomatic flexibility and Congress’ desire to maintain Ukraine’s military strength and the security of Europe.

This is also a NATO and transatlantic issue. The president’s repeated pleas for Europeans to take more responsibility for their own defence, including more ambitious targets for defence spending, has revealed significant disagreements over burden-sharing, US military involvement and the future of collective security. A Congress dominated by Democrats would provide European governments with some institutional comfort by amending the budget to increase the US military presence in NATO nations and to provide statutory guarantees for NATO, and it would hasten its review of potential cuts in US forces along NATO’s eastern flank and resist sudden unilateral changes to security cooperation. But the basic question still exists: will American commitments last beyond the tenure of a single president or a single election cycle? The short-term result is likely to boost congressional backing for transatlantic cooperation, but not necessarily end the credibility gap brought on by Trump’s erratic policy of alliance management and public statements.

This political shift in Washington will also have an impact on US-China relations. When it comes to Beijing, significant partisan polarisation is present in the area of technological limitations, export controls, military modernisation and Taiwan. Whereas bipartisan consensus remains in overall strategic competition. Thus, the Democrats’ ability to control Congress in the short-term is not likely to lead to a major shift in America’s competitive position on China. It may, however, alter the approaches and political agendas that are used to fight that competition, especially the “economic” aspects. More thoughtful consideration by Congress could limit unilateral tariffs made without the participation of Congress, incentivise more coordinated economic policy with allies and partners, and increase focus on cooperation with Asian partners like Japan, South Korea and Australia. The new political landscape in Washington offers China both challenges and opportunities for its long-term planning. It could be more difficult for the US to coordinate strategic policy on trade and technology, as well as to coordinate other policies, given the growing domestic polarisation over America’s future direction. At the same time, greater bipartisan monitoring could increase institutionalisation and reduce the impact of presidential preferences on other policies.

The significance of the midterm elections to India is that the outcome has placed the relationship between American domestic politics and the sustainability of the Indo-US strategic partnership in the spotlight. [Defence technology cooperation](https://defence.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/policy-partnerships/india-and-the-us-need-to-turn-defence-promises-into-hard-power/135493072), intelligence sharing, maritime security, Indo-Pacific and critical minerals and emerging technology continues to be central to the bilateral and regional interests of both nations. As Congress gets more involved in the debate, the issues of tariffs, immigration, H-1B visas, access to trade and diplomatic access and priorities could be more politically charged in congressional hearings and committees. The Democrats will question some of Trump’s economic and immigration policies on humanitarian and financial reasons, but not the strategic relationship with New Delhi.

Therefore, India should not equate the Republican Party’s electoral defeat with an automatic shift in the Trump administration’s policy towards India. New Delhi’s best interests will be to boost the engagement of both parties in Washington, including through think-tanks, Congress and Diaspora networks, diversify economic relationships away from the US and maintain strategic independence while understanding that the credibility of America’s global commitments will increasingly rely on domestic political consensus.
