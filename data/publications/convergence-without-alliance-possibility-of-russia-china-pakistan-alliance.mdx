South Asia’s security landscape has been shaped by the India–Pakistan rivalry and the involvement of external powers. Within this configuration, China and Pakistan are bound by [an “all-weather” strategic partnership](https://mofa.gov.pk/press-releases/joint-statement-between-the-peoples-republic-of-china-and-the-islamic-republic-of-pakistan-may-26-2026) anchored in the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), the pilot component of the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). Within Beijing’s [hierarchy of partnerships](https://merics.org/en/comment/marriage-convenience-strategic-partnership-china-russia-relations-and-fight-global), this relationship belongs to the second-highest, “all-weather” tier, below only China’s comprehensive strategic partnership of coordination with Russia, which the two governments have framed since 2022 as a friendship with “no limits”. Since 2014, when Russia’s annexation of Crimea accelerated its isolation from the West, Russia and Pakistan have moved from mutual neglect to a modest but durable partnership centred on [energy, connectivity and security cooperation](https://mofa.gov.pk/moscow-pakistan-russia-relations). These dynamics point to a weak but emerging Russia–China–Pakistan axis. Yet the term denotes neither a formal alliance nor fixed bloc politics, automatic support in crises or an institutionalised trilateral organisation. It operates instead as an asymmetrical and issue-based alignment.

Russia’s engagement with Pakistan and the CPEC illustrates a strategic balancing act in which [public endorsement](https://www.dawn.com/news/1302890) and selective bilateral cooperation coexist with [cautious distancing from formal participation](https://www.mid.ru/en/foreign_policy/news/1538683/). It seeks access to Gwadar and Karachi and explores CPEC-adjacent routes, but keeps its engagements formally outside the corridor’s umbrella. This low-risk, high-reward strategy widens Moscow’s options without assuming the corridor’s political and security costs.

The strongest driver of the Russia–China–Pakistan alignment is geo-economic convergence. Russia’s sanctions-driven reorientation increases its interest in Asian markets, alternative payment mechanisms and the International North–South Transport Corridor (INSTC), a multimodal route linking Russia and India via Iran. Pakistan needs energy and investment. China seeks to advance an “upgraded” CPEC beyond infrastructure and energy towards industry, agriculture, mining and development cooperation. In June 2026, [Pakistan signalled its intention to join the INSTC and supported linking it with Gwadar](https://pid.gov.pk/site/press_detail/32950), and thus with CPEC and the wider BRI. [The May 2026 China–Pakistan joint statement](https://mofa.gov.pk/press-releases/joint-statement-between-the-peoples-republic-of-china-and-the-islamic-republic-of-pakistan-may-26-2026) welcomed third-party participation in CPEC on terms agreed by Beijing and Islamabad. These developments widen the opening for Russia but do not amount to Russian accession. Pakistan Stream, a bilateral 1,100-kilometre gas-pipeline project linking LNG terminals near Karachi with Punjab, and proposed Russia–Pakistan freight links remain at the planning stage, illustrating the implementation gap.

Overlapping bilateral and multilateral security cooperation provides a second foundation. All three share concerns about Afghanistan’s instability, transnational terrorism, drug trafficking and threats to connectivity projects. In June 2026, [the bilateral Russia–Pakistan counterterrorism working group](https://mofa.gov.pk/press-releases/12th-round-of-the-pakistan-russia-joint-working-group-to-counter-international-terrorism) addressed Afghanistan-based threats, terrorist financing and recruitment, and coordination within the United Nations (UN) and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO). This bilateral track overlaps with SCO counterterrorism mechanisms involving all three states, but does not amount to a trilateral security structure. China–Pakistan security cooperation remains much deeper.

Several constraints make the alignment’s consolidation into a cohesive bloc unlikely. First, India remains Russia’s principal defence partner in South Asia. [New Delhi opposes CPEC](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003305132-4/india-china%E2%80%93pakistan-economic-corridor-agnieszka-nitza-makowska) primarily because it traverses disputed territory in Pakistan-administered Kashmir. Its other concerns include Gwadar’s possible militarisation as a Chinese naval base, neglected human-rights and environmental standards, and Beijing’s geopolitical leverage over India’s neighbours. Formal Russian participation would deepen Indian threat perceptions and regional polarisation. Second, the alignment is profoundly unequal. China is the senior economic and political partner. Russia remains militarily capable but sanctions-constrained. Analysts warn that dependence on China could reduce Moscow to a [junior partner](https://www.iss.europa.eu/publications/analysis/dependence-gap-russia-china-relations), although Russia does not publicly use this framing. Pakistan is the weakest actor in the axis, though pivotal because of its location, nuclear status and the CPEC. Moreover, [Pakistan’s facilitation of US–Iran talks](https://lkyspp.nus.edu.sg/cag/publications/center-publications/publication-article/detail/china-and-india-amid-the-iran-war--the-implications-of-pakistan-s-mediating-role) has produced a temporary but politically meaningful elevation of its standing and diplomatic utility, without changing the triangle’s material balance.

Third, Pakistan’s internal instability, militancy in Balochistan and criticism of CPEC’s “colonial” features create additional costs. Russia’s reduced post-2022 investment capacity limits its contribution to discounted commodities and energy expertise. Finally, [Pakistan practises “non-alignment 2.0”](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/280/article/994826), a non-ideological foreign-policy posture that avoids “camp politics” and seeks strategic autonomy by prioritising national interest over bloc loyalty. In Pakistan, this posture takes the form of hedging under constraint, as a fragile state uses crisis management to maximise manoeuvrability among China, Russia, the West and Gulf partners. Were the axis to harden into a formalised bloc and extend beyond its issue-based scope, it could narrow Islamabad’s room for manoeuvre by distancing it from Washington, the EU and their allies.

The most plausible outcome is therefore alignment without alliance, a flexible, transactional and China-centred configuration focused on energy, connectivity, Afghanistan and selective opposition to Western dominance. If consolidated, it may intensify India’s threat perceptions, encourage regional bloc formation and contribute to arms racing. For now, its capacity to generate threat perceptions exceeds its material cohesion and ability to reshape South Asia. The axis remains implementation-light and strategically constrained.
