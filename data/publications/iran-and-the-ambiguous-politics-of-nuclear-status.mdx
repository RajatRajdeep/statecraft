In August 2026, the U.S. Ambassador to the United Nations, Mike Waltz [circulated a false claim]( https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/16/business/iran-nuclear-false-claims.html) that Iran had declared itself a nuclear power. The claim falsely quoted Ahmad Vahidi, the Commander-in-Chief of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, as saying that “as long as US and Israel are owning nuclear weapons, we will keep working on it for national security. If they dispose, we will dispose.” The false post seems to imply a motivation or intention to build nuclear weapons rather than a declaration of Iran’s nuclear capabilities. But in noting that the post “declared” Iran a “nuclear power,” the reporting on the incident assumed that declaring an _intention_ to weaponise is the same thing as declaring a _current_ status.

More curious still is that Iran [_did_ indeed declare itself a nuclear state]( https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna35343465)—back in 2010. The former Iranian President, Mahmoud Ahmadinejad did so when Iran first possessed the advanced ability to enrich uranium. No weapon, just enriched uranium.

This incident is emblematic of a long-ensuing politics around a largely taken-for-granted question in the global nuclear regime—what does it mean to be a nuclear state? In my recent book, [_After Fission: Recognition and Contestation in the Atomic Age_]( https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/after-fission/F63C0DAADDFEA6554AD827A79C5D4E8D), I argue that the answer to this question is more a matter of status and recognition than of capability. I conceptualise nuclear status as distinct from nuclear capability, arguing that the former entails a separate process of social recognition but is often collapsed with the latter. Complicating the relationship between nuclear status and nuclear capability illuminates the myopia of confusing what Iran _has_ with what the Iranians _want_.

The history and politics of nuclear status cannot be understood without reference to the landmark 1970 Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT). The NPT is often lauded as one of the most successful components of the global nonproliferation regime. But it is also structured around a hierarchy. It has different legal obligations for two categories of states: “nuclear weapon states” (NWS) and “non-nuclear weapon states” (NNWS). The NWS commit to not provide NNWS with nuclear weapons or sensitive technologies that can be used to build a nuclear weapon, while the NNWS commit not to build nuclear weapons of their own. 

More important is the way the treaty differentiates a NWS from a NNWS—a NWS is any country that exploded a nuclear device prior to January 1, 1967. The definition may appear neat and precise, and yet the reality it seeks to capture is complicated. The road to a nuclear weapons arsenal is, in fact, a road, rather than an on/off (nuclear/non-nuclear) switch that the treaty suggests. In other words, it is not always clear which point in the road symbolises a clear nuclear status and at what point the destination of nuclear weapons possession becomes inevitable. From the perspective of the treaty, a nuclear weapon is perhaps not even a weapon until it is tested.

_After Fission_ examines the history of four nuclear programmes started after the NPT’s 1967 cutoff: India, Israel, Iran, and North Korea. Some of these states have found ways around the hurdle of the NPT’s binary categorisation. Israel has pursued a policy of [strategic ambiguity]( https://cup.columbia.edu/book/israel-and-the-bomb/9780231500098/) (_amimut_ in Hebrew), continually denying that the country has nuclear weapons, while maintaining a small but well-documented nuclear arsenal. And India has a very public nuclear arsenal built in contravention to the treaty’s terms and yet has successfully been recognised by the United States as a “[responsible nuclear state]( https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2005/07/a-nuclear-triumph-for-india).”

In this international regime marked by distinct categories, states seek not only the security advantage of an advanced nuclear programme but also navigate how their nuclear programme is seen, categorised, and recognised by the rest of the world. The gap between the NPT’s rigid categories and the more diffuse and complicated material terrain of nuclear technology invites states to either explicitly challenge (Iran and North Korea) or subtly subvert this system (Israel and India).

What does this distinctive history of nuclear governance mean for policy and diplomacy? _After Fission_ exhorts U.S. leadership to negotiate around nuclear status rather than its typical focus on capabilities. When the Iranians contend that they do not seek a nuclear weapon and keep pursuing a robust nuclear capacity, they are doing something different than just hedging or, worse yet, lying. Iranian actions stem from the peculiar legal separation between nuclear and non-nuclear states and the status anxiety that comes from being in a liminal space between the two. Iran does not yet possess a nuclear weapon but places great importance and national pride in its nuclear programme—it is one of the few countries in the world with a National Nuclear Technology Day annual celebration. In this context, what ends up mattering even more than the objective truth of a country’s nuclear capabilities is the subjective status evaluations that occur through diplomacy. 

Indeed, the (unintended) innovation of the 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) was that it found a way to limit Iranian nuclear capability _without_ limiting its desire for nuclear status. The JCPOA’s provisions [severely curtailed Iran’s nuclear programme]( https://www.cfr.org/backgrounders/what-iran-nuclear-deal). It forbade Iran from producing highly-enriched uranium and instead capped enrichment levels at 3.67 per cent, far from the 70-90 per cent required for the production of nuclear weapons. It limited the number of centrifuges in which uranium enrichment could be performed, and established a stringent inspection regime with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA).

But the JCPOA did not completely dismantle Iran’s nuclear programme. This fact was often deemed a major downfall of the deal but it was, in many ways, its most important innovation. Allowing the Iranians to keep a limited enrichment capacity meant that they could sell the deal domestically. The Iranians maintained that the JCPOA recognised their right to a peaceful nuclear programme. And like the 123 Agreement did for India, the JCPOA opened the door to a potential change in Iran’s international status in the global nuclear regime from a pariah and rogue to a potential member. 

The JCPOA was an attempt at accommodation not unlike deals struck in the past to prevent conflict with countries who felt that they were getting the short end of the nonproliferation stick. The Obama administration justified the deal by noting that its terms were based on [verification and not on trust]( https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/2015/07/14/statement-president-iran). Surely, the deal itself did not resolve decades of U.S.-Iranian enmity. But the unstated payoff was less about ensuring, beyond a doubt, that Iran never builds a nuclear weapon and more about changing what Iran represents in the world.

This diplomatic history points in the opposite direction to the logic of regime change as a solution for dealing with nuclear risk. But isn’t there good reason to assume that an authoritarian regime like Iran’s has the worst intentions when it comes to nuclear technology? Isn’t accommodation simply appeasement? The Islamic Republic poses an affront to free and open society everywhere. The leaders of the revolutionary regime have clung to a solipsistic, and yet Manichean, worldview that prevents the flourishing of Iranian society. But the international struggle over Iran’s nuclear programme has only served to deepen these tendencies. Even more, Iran’s nuclear policies cannot be neatly extracted from the hierarchical context and uneven rules in which the policies were forged.
