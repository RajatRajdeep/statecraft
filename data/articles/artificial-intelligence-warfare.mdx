The battlefield of the mid-twenty-first century will be shaped by artificial intelligence in ways that existing strategic frameworks are poorly equipped to address. From autonomous drone swarms to AI-assisted targeting systems, from predictive logistics to cyber operations guided by machine learning, the military applications of AI are compressing decision timelines, blurring the line between human and machine agency, and introducing new failure modes that strategists are only beginning to understand.

## The Autonomy Spectrum

"Lethal autonomous weapons systems" (LAWS) is a contested term covering a wide spectrum of human-machine configurations. At one end are automated systems — missile defence batteries, for example — that exercise autonomous action within tightly defined parameters with human authorisation. At the other end are fully autonomous systems capable of selecting and engaging targets without human intervention.

Most current military AI applications fall somewhere between these poles: humans retain nominal decision authority but exercise it under conditions — compressed timelines, degraded communications, cognitive overload — that render meaningful oversight increasingly notional.

The drone warfare that has defined conflicts from Nagorno-Karabakh to Ukraine illustrates the trajectory. AI-enabled drones that can navigate without GPS, identify targets using computer vision, and operate in swarms that saturate defensive systems are already deployed capabilities, not theoretical futures.

## Great Power Competition in Military AI

The United States, China, and Russia are all investing heavily in military AI, and all are pursuing strategies that reflect their respective strengths and vulnerabilities. American investment is focused on maintaining qualitative superiority through the Department of Defense's Joint AI Center and Project Maven. China's Military-Civil Fusion doctrine seeks to leverage its commercial AI sector's capabilities for PLA modernisation. Russia has pursued autonomous systems as a force multiplier for its quantitatively inferior conventional forces.

The strategic risks of competitive military AI development are several. Speed of development may outpace the development of fail-safes, red lines, and escalation management mechanisms. Systems trained on historical data may perform unpredictably in novel operational environments. AI vulnerabilities to adversarial manipulation — feeding systems false data to produce incorrect outputs — create new attack surfaces. And automated decision-making in crisis scenarios may compress the time available for political leaders to de-escalate, with potentially catastrophic consequences.

## The Governance Gap

International humanitarian law (IHL) requires that belligerents distinguish between combatants and civilians, apply proportionality in the use of force, and exercise military necessity judgement. These requirements presuppose a human decision-maker with the cognitive and moral capacity to make such judgements. Whether AI systems can satisfy these legal requirements — and who bears responsibility when they fail — are questions to which international law currently has no satisfactory answers.

Efforts to negotiate a treaty on LAWS within the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) have stalled, largely due to the opposition of major military powers that have no interest in constraining capabilities they are developing. The resulting governance gap means that the deployment of increasingly autonomous weapons is proceeding without agreed international standards.

## India's Position and Interests

India's engagement with the military AI debate has been cautious. India has participated in CCW discussions while maintaining ambiguity about its own development trajectory. India's defence technology ecosystem — a mix of DRDO indigenous development, private sector partnerships, and foreign collaboration — is developing drone and AI capabilities, though significant gaps in AI talent and computing infrastructure remain.

India's strategic interest lies in ensuring that AI governance frameworks do not crystallise at a point that locks in the advantages of current technology leaders. More immediately, India faces the practical challenge of adversaries — Pakistan and China — integrating AI into their own military systems, requiring a corresponding Indian response that remains coherent with its own legal and ethical commitments.

The development of norms around military AI is an arena where middle powers with credible security concerns and democratic governance traditions — India among them — have both the interest and the standing to shape the emerging order.
