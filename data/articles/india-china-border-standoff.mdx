The friction points along the Line of Actual Control (LAC) in Eastern Ladakh represent more than a territorial dispute. They are symptomatic of a fundamental contest between two rising powers with incompatible visions of their own strategic space.

## The Galwan Watershed

The violence of June 2020, when Indian and Chinese soldiers clashed in Galwan Valley, killing 20 Indian soldiers and an undisclosed number of Chinese personnel, shattered four decades of relative peace along the contested border. More significantly, it exploded the carefully cultivated diplomatic fiction that India and China could "agree to disagree" on the boundary question while pursuing broader economic and political engagement.

What distinguished Galwan from earlier friction episodes — Doklam in 2017, Depsang in 2013 — was its intensity and the speed with which it hardened positions on both sides. India's rapid military build-up in the aftermath, its acceleration of border infrastructure projects previously slowed by diplomatic caution, and New Delhi's willingness to impose economic costs on Chinese companies signalled a qualitative shift in how India perceived the threat.

## PLA's Infrastructure Offensive

Chinese military infrastructure expansion along the LAC has been systematic and sustained over two decades. The People's Liberation Army has constructed hundreds of kilometres of all-weather roads, airfields, logistics depots, and dual-use villages in what Beijing terms "model border defence villages." This infrastructure is not merely defensive — it reduces Chinese mobilisation times and enables rapid force projection across multiple sectors simultaneously.

India's response, while accelerating, faces structural disadvantages rooted in its own strategic culture. Historically, India's approach to border infrastructure was governed by a perverse logic: that good roads would help China more than India. This assumption has been abandoned, but catching up requires years of sustained investment and political will.

## The Disengagement Illusion

Partial disengagement at specific friction points — Pangong Tso, Gogra-Hot Springs — has been presented as diplomatic progress. In a narrow military sense, pulling back forward-deployed forces reduces the risk of accidental escalation. But disengagement without restoration of the April 2020 status quo ante leaves China in possession of strategic gains it seized during the COVID-disrupted period.

The broader problem is structural. India and China have not agreed on where the LAC actually runs in many sectors. Without a formal boundary agreement, there is no stable endpoint to "disengage" toward. Each episode of friction is therefore a negotiation over facts on the ground rather than a deviation from agreed norms.

## Strategic Implications

For India, the lessons of Galwan have accelerated several policy shifts. Greater emphasis on border infrastructure, a more hardheaded approach to economic dependencies on China, tighter alignment with like-minded partners in the Quad framework, and a recognition that maintaining deterrence requires visible military capacity — not diplomatic goodwill alone.

Yet India must also manage the risk of strategic overextension. Simultaneous confrontation with Pakistan and China — the two-front threat that Indian planners have long worried about — is not a scenario New Delhi can approach with equanimity. Managing tensions with China while sustaining relations that prevent active collusion between Beijing and Islamabad remains one of India's most complex diplomatic challenges.

The Himalayan standoff will not be resolved quickly. China's patience is structural; its territorial ambitions in the western sector serve specific operational and strategic purposes. For India, the immediate imperative is to ensure that the costs China pays for continued salami-slicing remain higher than the gains — and that the international community understands what is at stake.
