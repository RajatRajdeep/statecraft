The Sahel — the semi-arid zone stretching from Senegal to Sudan across the southern edge of the Sahara — is the world's fastest-growing theatre of jihadist violence. Groups affiliated with al-Qaeda (Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin, JNIM) and the Islamic State (ISGS) have exploited state fragility, interethnic conflict, and the collapse of French counterterrorism operations to expand their territorial control and civilian death toll.

## The French Withdrawal and Its Aftermath

Operation Barkhane, France's decade-long counterterrorism mission in the Sahel, was one of the most sustained Western military engagements in Africa since the Cold War. At its peak, it deployed 5,500 troops across five countries, conducted thousands of operations, and eliminated several senior jihadist commanders.

Yet Barkhane failed in its fundamental objective: to create conditions for sustainable security that could be maintained by Sahelian governments and armed forces without continued French support. The reasons are structural rather than operational. French forces could kill jihadists; they could not address the governance failures, ethnic grievances, and economic marginalisation that made those same communities receptive to jihadist recruitment.

The wave of military coups in Mali (2021), Burkina Faso (2022), and Niger (2023) — all overthrowing French-aligned governments amid popular anger partly directed at France's continued military presence — effectively ended the Barkhane mission. The junta governments that replaced them turned instead to Russia's Wagner Group (now Africa Corps), whose methods are more brutal and whose strategic objectives serve Russian influence projection rather than Sahelian stability.

## Wagner, Russia, and the Security Vacuum

Russia's insertion into the Sahelian security vacuum through private military companies represents a calculated strategic gambit. Wagner/Africa Corps offers juntas something French forces could not: unconditional military support without demands for democratic governance or human rights compliance. In exchange, Russia receives diplomatic leverage, access to natural resources, and an opportunity to weaken the Western-dominated security architecture in West Africa.

The results, where Wagner has operated, have been predictably catastrophic for civilians. The Moura massacre in Mali (2022), where Wagner and Malian army forces killed several hundred people over five days, is the most documented atrocity. Multiple credible accounts describe similar patterns across areas of Wagner deployment.

Yet from the juntas' perspective, the calculus is not irrational. French forces did not prevent coups; Wagner will actively protect the governments that employ it. In the narrow logic of regime survival, the trade-off has some appeal.

## The Strategic Implications for India

India's engagement with African security may appear tangential, but the Sahel's instability has consequences that extend well beyond the region. Jihadist networks operating from West Africa have demonstrated the capacity to project violence globally. The Islamic State's expansion into Mozambique, and the network connections between Sahelian groups and movements in East Africa, suggest a trajectory of southward and eastward expansion.

More directly, the Sahel crisis tests the durability of the international counterterrorism consensus established after 2001. If jihadist movements can consolidate territorial control in a region while international attention is absorbed by Ukraine and the Indo-Pacific, it validates a strategic patience that other groups will observe and potentially emulate.

India's growing engagement with Africa — through the India-Africa Forum Summits, defence partnerships, and development cooperation — gives it both interests and instruments in the region. A more active Indian role in supporting legitimate governance and security capacity, consistent with African-owned frameworks, would serve both India's global partnerships and its own long-term security interests.
